# Reviewer 1
Review Report for Sustainable Cities and Society Manuscript Number: SCSI-D-26-09591R1

## Overall Comment
1. General Assessment The authors have made substantial revisions and have satisfactorily addressed most of the concerns raised in the initial review. The revised manuscript provides a more coherent spatial allocation method, additional comparisons with official damage reports, improved sensitivity analyses, and clearer explanations of the engineering and mortality-risk assumptions. The distinction between modeled planning estimates and observed disaster impacts has also been strengthened. The revisions have improved the methodological transparency and overall credibility of the study. Nevertheless, several limitations concerning spatial allocation, population exposure assumptions, and the interpretation of health-risk estimates warrant further clarification before publication. 
2. Comments


**Response:**
Thank you for your positive assessment and constructive suggestions. The revision clarifies the role of the primary allocation, the scope of demographic sensitivity, the illustrative mortality scenario, and the implications of reporting updates for emergency resource planning. Detailed responses follow.

## Comment 1
(1) The additional comparisons with official damage reports are useful. However, the primary allocation still shows substantial spatial disagreement (total-variation distance = 0.5845), while the shaking-based alternatives demonstrate closer agreement. The authors should briefly clarify why the distance-based allocation remains the preferred primary scenario despite these findings and ensure that its limitations are reflected in the conclusions.


**Response:**
Thank you for this comment. Section 2.4 now explains the retained primary as a parsimonious reference for linked planning comparisons, and the Conclusion clarifies its preliminary-screening role relative to the tested shaking alternatives. The revised passages state:

"We retain this distance-based specification as a parsimonious reference for linked planning comparisons, without the nearest-station assignments and transferred vulnerability curves used in the shaking-based alternatives."
(Page 10, Lines 191–193)

"The distance-based primary serves as a reference for preliminary screening, without implying greater geographic accuracy than the tested shaking alternatives."
(Page 34, Lines 746–748)

## Comment 2
(2) The revised older-population allocation and demographic sensitivity analysis represent meaningful improvements. However, the close agreement between alternative demographic allocations does not test whether older households experience different probabilities of housing damage. This distinction should remain explicit when interpreting the robustness of the estimated exposed population.


**Response:**
Thank you for this clarification. Section 3.3 now makes explicit that agreement between the demographic allocations is conditional on the shared housing-loss rule and does not test age-specific housing-damage probabilities. The revised passage states:

"Residual-capacity demographic weighting yields 202.185 exposed older residents, with a maximum rank shift of one. Agreement between the demographic allocations is conditional on the same housing-loss rule and does not test whether older households have different probabilities of housing damage."
(Page 22, Lines 471–474)

## Comment 3
(3) The additional mortality-risk sensitivity analyses and discussion of parameter transferability are appreciated. Nevertheless, the central 5.31% mortality-burden increase remains dependent on healtheffect parameters transferred from Ontario nursing homes. The authors should ensure that this result is consistently presented as an illustrative scenario rather than a locally validated health effect. The hypothetical no-increment scenario should not be interpreted as independent evidence of robustness.


**Response:**
Thank you for this comment. The Abstract now labels the 5.31% contrast as an illustrative scenario using transferred health parameters. Section 3.5 retains its explanation of the no-increment benchmark as an algebraic result, not independent evidence of robustness. The revised Abstract states:

"With the same outdoor heat sequence in both states, loss of effective cooling raises the prefecture-weighted modeled 30-day mortality burden by 5.31% in an illustrative scenario using transferred health parameters."
(Page 1, Lines 18–20)

## Comment 4
(4) The retrospective analysis indicates that the later reported housing-damage total is approximately 17.95 times the initial value. This substantial difference highlights the sensitivity of estimated cooling and electricity requirements to the timing and completeness of disaster reporting. The practical implications of this limitation should be emphasized when discussing the applicability of the framework to emergency resource planning.


**Response:**
Thank you for highlighting this practical implication. Section 4.3 now connects the 17.95-fold retrospective increase in the functional-loss constraint to the need to update resource estimates as damage assessments become more complete. It also recommends staged preparation with repeated local checks of placement and power availability. The revised text states:

"In the retrospective test, the 17.95-fold increase in the loss constraint illustrates how reporting updates can change the scale of resource requirements. Emergency planning should therefore update demand estimates as assessments become more complete and use staged preparation with repeated local checks of placement and power availability."
(Pages 28–29, Lines 616–620)

# Reviewer 3

## Overall Comment
I thank the Authors for their careful response and for the substantive changes introduced. The main concerns raised in the first round have been addressed satisfactorily. The boundary of the exposed population is now explicit in the Abstract and in Sections 1, 2.3 and 3.1; the assumption underlying Eq. 3 is explicitly stated, accompanied by a sensitivity analysis of the demographic allocation, and acknowledged as a limitation. The transferability of the parameters from Katz et al. (2026) is discussed with comparative literature and distinguished from statistical uncertainty. The description of the engineering parameters has been expanded, with reference to Table S1, and the title “Spatial Screening Framework” is consistent with the outputs actually produced.
I particularly appreciate the replacement of the central surface with a coherent scenario, clearly distinguished from the median-based sensitivity analysis, and the presentation of comparisons with official reports as descriptive evidence without treating them as independent validation. I consider the revision satisfactory. The following two suggestions are optional and are intended solely to improve clarity; they are not conditions for publication.


**Response:**
Thank you for your positive assessment and helpful optional suggestions. The Conclusion clarifies the primary allocation's screening role. The Abstract identifies the housing-damage snapshot date, and Section 3.3 reports the retrospective population and electricity estimates. Detailed responses follow.

## Comment 1
o1 – Interpretation of the primary allocation:
Section 3.3 shows that, in the comparison with the September 29 reports, the primary allocation yields a rank correlation of 0.7252 and a top-five overlap of 3/5, compared with 0.7732–0.8127 and 4/5 for the shaking-based alternatives. This difference is appropriately discussed in Section 4.5. The Authors may wish to briefly reiterate in the Conclusions that the primary specification serves as a reference for preliminary screening, without implying greater geographic accuracy than the alternatives tested, and that local priorities require field verification.


**Response:**
Thank you for this suggestion. The Conclusion presents the primary allocation as a reference for preliminary screening and directs responders to verify placement and cooling performance:

"The distance-based primary serves as a reference for preliminary screening, without implying greater geographic accuracy than the tested shaking alternatives. Its outputs identify where responders should verify placement and cooling performance and how much demand-side capacity may be required."
(Page 34, Lines 746–749)

## Comment 2
o2 – Temporal reference of the estimated quantities:
The retrospective test in Section 3.3 raises the functional-loss constraint to 5,448.5 units, 17.95 times the initial value of 303.5. The Authors may wish to identify explicitly in the Abstract the August 1, 2026 snapshot to which the central quantities refer, and to clarify in Section 3.3 that updating the total also changes the scale of the demographic and energy estimates. If already available, the corresponding retrospective values for residents aged 65 or older, peak power and daily electricity could be reported concisely. No new analysis is requested.


**Response:**
Thank you for these suggestions. The Abstract now identifies the August 1, 2026 housing-damage snapshot underlying the central estimates. Section 3.3 adds the existing retrospective estimates for older residents, peak power and daily electricity under unchanged spatial weights, bounded demographic allocation and Central engineering parameters. The revised passages are:

"The central allocation, based on the August 1, 2026 housing-damage snapshot, contains 303.5 functionally lost residences and approximately 202 affected residents aged 65 or older."
(Page 1, Lines 14–16)

"In the total-only retrospective test, the later snapshot raises this constraint to 5,448.5, or 17.95 times the early value. With unchanged spatial weights, bounded demographic allocation and Central engineering parameters, the September 24, 2026 snapshot yields a cooling-assessment population of 3,627.102 residents aged 65 or older, requiring 510.333 kW of peak cooling power and 9,185.999 kWh of electricity per day."
(Page 21, Lines 447–452)
